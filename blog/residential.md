Residential structures are rarely difficult to analyse in isolation. The loads
are modest and well understood, and the spans are short. What makes the work
demanding is everything around the structure: keeping floor zones shallow enough
to hold storey heights, threading services through without cutting structure,
and getting the same detail to work several hundred times over.

## Repetition changes the economics

On an eight unit building, a slightly inefficient slab costs very little. On a
one hundred and eighty unit development, the same inefficiency is repeated across
every floor of every block, and it becomes one of the largest single costs in the
frame.

This is why large residential schemes justify a level of optimisation that would
be wasted on a small one. Reducing slab thickness by twenty millimetres is not
worth pursuing on a single block. Across a large development it changes the
concrete volume, the reinforcement tonnage, the foundation loads, and often the
overall building height.

The same logic applies to the structural grid. A layout that lets walls and
columns stack cleanly from roof to foundation is worth more than one that suits
any individual floor plate, because transfer structures are expensive and every
one of them interrupts the repetition that makes the scheme efficient.

## Prefabricated concrete in housing

Prefabricated concrete suits residential work for the same reason: the elements
repeat. Once a wall panel or floor plank is designed and the mould exists,
producing more of them is cheap and fast.

The trade off is that decisions get locked early. Openings, service penetrations
and fixings have to be resolved before casting, because altering a finished
element on site is slow and sometimes not possible at all. Coordination with the
architect and the services engineer has to run ahead of the structural design
rather than alongside it, which is a change in how the project is sequenced.

Connections do the work that would otherwise be done by monolithic concrete.
Panel to panel joints, floor to wall bearings and the tying arrangements that
give the building robustness all have to be designed explicitly, and they govern
how the structure behaves under accidental actions.

## Standards and tools

Design follows the Eurocodes as adopted in Germany, DIN EN 1990 through 1998,
including the requirements for structural robustness and tying that apply to
residential buildings. Analysis is carried out in SOFiSTiK, Dlubal and RFEM, with
execution drawings produced in Nemetschek and AutoCAD.
